The Story

Sobriety isn't a Straight Line

I know people say you can't get addicted to cannabis, but they're wrong. You can literally become addicted to anything: drugs, alcohol, shopping, food, phone games, you name it. I started using cannabis when I went to college 15 years ago, and it quickly became my main coping mechanism for the anxiety and depression I've carried since childhood.

Like most addictions, it started because I genuinely enjoyed it and thought it was good for my mental health. But I eventually realized how much it had stagnated my growth and limited my ability to improve my life. For years, I tried cutting back or quitting cold turkey, but I would always end up back in the clutches of addiction.

Things changed when I was finally diagnosed with ADHD at 31. It turned out I'd been self-medicating a brain that worked differently than I'd been told my whole life. The right medication and therapy got me to a place where I could truly give sober Beck a fighting chance.

When starting out this journey, my therapist Julie told me that it takes a full year for people to successfully overcome an addiction. Her reasoning was that you need to experience sobriety across all the seasons, holidays, events, and days you move through in a year. That way, it's harder for the brain to fight back by insisting you need the substance to get through certain times. For me, I couldn't imagine going camping with my friends without weed, because that's where I always enjoyed it the most.

That idea is exactly why this tool counts collective sober days across the whole year, season by season, instead of tracking a single "quit date." Because going sober for a whole year is much easier said than done. When I first heard it, I was dumbstruck. I couldn't stay sober for a few days, let alone 365 of them. Couldn't I just do a month and find balance? The idea of actually quitting terrified me, and I've felt like a failure every time I've tried and relapsed. But I'm not a failure, because healing from addiction is not a straight line. More than half of people who try to quit an addiction relapse. It's a core part of the sobriety journey, not the end of it. Letting go of the all-or-nothing thinking is what helped me start walking this path in the first place, and it's what's helped me stick with it.

So far, this approach has hands down been the most effective way I've dealt with my own struggles. I am so proud of the collective sober days I've achieved in 2026. I wouldn't have been able to do it if I never offered myself forgiveness or acceptance when I made the very human mistake of slipping up or thinking I could smoke just a little. I decided to share my story in raw, unedited journal entries for all you dear internet strangers to read because perhaps you will connect to my story. I hope something in my ramblings sticks with you and helps you on your own path to sobriety.

If you want to try my tool for yourself, for whatever addiction is limiting your potential, you're welcome to it!

  1. Download the starter template from my GitHub repo (a file called starter-template.html). It's the same tool you're looking at now, stripped of my entries and ready for yours.
  2. Try it right away by just opening the file in your browser. It runs on your own computer, no account needed. Name what you're stepping back from, pick a start date, and check in.
  3. Your journal saves itself in your browser as you go, private to you. Nothing is uploaded anywhere. To keep a permanent copy that won't disappear if you clear your browser, tap Export backup every so often. That downloads a file with all your entries. Save it somewhere you'll find it again, like your notes, cloud drive, or email.

A note on phones: this works most smoothly on a computer. You can use it on a phone, but opening a downloaded file is harder there. And phone browsers sometimes clear saved data on their own, so if you go mobile, export a backup often.
